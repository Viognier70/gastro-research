Aristotelian knowledge
Three forms of knowledge

Aristotle identified three forms of knowledge. Understanding which type a study represents helps you apply it appropriately in professional practice.

Episteme
ἐπιστήμη — theoretical, scientific knowledge
Universal truths and scientific principles — knowledge that can be proven, replicated, and taught. Apply with high confidence across contexts.
Sommelier example: Research shows retronasal olfaction accounts for ~80% of perceived flavor. You can explain with scientific authority why a guest with a cold experiences wine so differently — and use it to guide service decisions with confidence.
Techne
τέχνη — craft knowledge, skilled practice
Practical skill that develops through deliberate practice and repetition. Lives in the hands, the nose, the palate — not in textbooks alone.
Sommelier example: Studies on sensory panel training show that 3 blind tastings per week over 6 months significantly improves grape variety identification. This is craft knowledge — your nose learns by doing, not by reading about it.
Phronesis
φρόνησις — practical wisdom, contextual judgment
The wisdom to act well in a specific situation — reading context, adapting to the guest, making sound judgments under uncertainty. Cannot be fully taught, only cultivated.
Sommelier example: Research on guest-sommelier dynamics shows that guests who feel "tested" order cheaper wine and enjoy the experience less. Phronesis is knowing when to share your expertise — and when to simply listen and serve.
